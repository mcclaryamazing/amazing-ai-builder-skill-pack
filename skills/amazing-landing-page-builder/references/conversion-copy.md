# Conversion Copy: Give The Reader A Reason To Buy

Read before substantial drafting or editorial review. This reference applies the bold-copy principles from Killer Copywriting and the buyer/offer discipline from Killer Landing Pages. It is self-contained; neither skill is an installation dependency.

## Don’t play it safe. Safe doesn’t sell.

Make the benefit of ownership vivid and the cost of postponement meaningful. A technically complete product summary is raw material, not the finished sales argument. Give the writer permission to persuade through recognizable situations, emotional stakes, future pacing, surprise, contrast and specificity. “Safe” means timid or interchangeable, not accurate. The most alarming wording is not automatically the strongest wording.

Start with the buyer's existing desire. Keep asking why the practical benefit matters until you reach an emotional payoff: confidence, control, comfort, pride, belonging, relief or anticipation. Use supplied customer language when available. Put inferred motivation in strategy notes as a hypothesis, then write naturally to that audience.

Build each important argument through feature → practical change → emotional significance. These are thinking steps, not a repeated three-sentence template. Explain why this product matters even without a discount. Then make the actual offer easy to choose.

## Use The Brief; Do Not Audit The Seller

Treat user-supplied product details, customer material, news context and offer terms as the working facts. Accuracy, substantiation and legal approval belong to the user. This workflow does not require a claim ledger, proof-status taxonomy, evidence request list, claim sensitivity score or independent fact-checking pass. Do not add one through the review rubric or another bundled reference.

Keep supplied facts and quotations faithful; do not invent missing facts or turn an imagined scene into a customer testimonial. Ask only about a concrete conflict or missing decision that materially changes the page. Otherwise omit the missing detail and keep writing. If the user requests research, perform it within that scope; new factual information introduced by the writer needs an actual source. Available proof earns belief; it is not a license gate for using the owner's brief.

## Make Possibilities Feel Real

Preparation does not require a prediction. A buyer can picture a dark hallway, an awkward airport search, an uncomfortable workday or a dinner they would enjoy hosting without a forecast or probability estimate.

Open a scenario with “Picture…”, “Imagine…” or “If…” when needed. Once the frame is clear, tell it naturally. Choose a few recognizable details, then connect the product to the practical task. Do not keep adding “possibly,” “hypothetically” and “may.” Do not append “no incident is forecast” to a passage that never claimed one was.

Use a useful progression, adapted to the product:

**Recognizable situation → why it matters → gap in the current approach → product's role → reason to believe → offer → action.**

The emotional destination is agency. Show what the reader can do, enjoy or improve. Avoid shame, helplessness and an escalating pile of unrelated disasters. Positive anticipation is as useful as avoiding frustration.

## Make Waiting A Decision

The alternative to buying is often continuing the current frustration, losing time, settling for discomfort, missing enjoyment or improvising later. Make that alternative concrete. A useful reason now can come from the buyer's life rather than a promotion.

- Preparation: “Put a light within reach before you have to find your way in the dark.”
- Convenience: “Give your keys a home before tomorrow's rush.”
- Enjoyment: “Set the table for the dinner you keep meaning to host.”
- Promotion: use the seller's actual deadline, if one exists. Do not invent one to energize the close.

## Sharpen The Whole Page

| Page element | Conversion job | Weak habit to replace |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H1 | Make a specific benefit, situation or curiosity worth reading; make a listicle's count and promise obvious | Tiny listicle eyebrow over a generic brand slogan |
| Opening | Create recognition and a buying question; connect promptly to the named product | Long scene-setting or defensive qualifications |
| Numbered reason | Develop a distinct reason to own or choose the product | Numbered specs or seven versions of one benefit |
| Explanation | Show how the product changes a real task or experience | Technical details without “so what?” |
| Customer material | Make the same benefit tangible in the customer's words | A detached wall of praise or conflating variants |
| Visual | Show the use, difference, detail or desired experience at this point | Decorative image with no persuasive purpose |
| Offer | Help the reader select a clearly named package and understand its value | Discounts substituting for product desire |
| FAQ | Resolve an actual remaining buying hesitation | Invented objections about the marketer's credibility |
| CTA | Name the next action and connect it to the payoff | Generic “Learn More” when a concrete choice is available |
| Close | Bring the core benefit and cost of postponing back to a decision | A summary with no reason to act |

## Before And After Examples

Illustrative wording below demonstrates craft, not product specifications or customer evidence. Adapt the mechanism to the supplied brief.

**Preparedness**

Weak: “A flashlight may be useful during an unexpected outage.”

Stronger: “If the lights went out tonight, would you know exactly where to reach? Picture the hallway going dark as everyone gets ready for bed. You need to find the dog, check the stairs and get the family settled. Put a dedicated light within reach before that becomes your first problem.”

Connect immediately to the named lighting product, its supplied capability and the actual package. Do not turn this general scene into a performance claim or predict an outage.

**Travel organization**

Weak: “Multiple compartments help organize your belongings.”

Stronger: “Your boarding pass is on your phone. Your phone is somewhere in your bag. Give the things you reach for most a place you can find without emptying everything at the gate.”

Then explain the actual pocket layout and what fits. The emotional benefit is composure and ease.

**Aspirational home purchase**

Weak: “Beautiful dinnerware for any occasion.”

Stronger: “Make Tuesday feel worth setting the table for.”

Develop the pleasure of an ordinary meal using the product's real design, materials and care details. Boldness can come from a fresh point of view rather than anxiety.

## Write And Challenge The Draft

Use the legends as complementary working lenses, not a list of names:

- Schwartz: which existing desire and level of awareness does the opening meet?
- Ogilvy: which concrete product truth makes the big idea distinctive?
- Halbert: why is this offer worth acting on for this buyer?
- Hopkins/Bencivenga: what explanation, detail, demonstration or supplied customer experience makes the promise believable?
- Caples: which headline communicates the strongest specific self-interest or curiosity?
- Sugarman: what makes each sentence earn the next; where does the momentum stop?
- Edwards: what better experience can the buyer picture, and what action gets them closer?

Read the H1, subhead, reason headings and CTA alone. They should communicate a persuasive argument. Then read the prose aloud for human rhythm, specificity and emotional movement. Develop weak summaries; remove unnecessary retreats. Preserve useful reinforcement where the reader is deciding. The final review evaluates desire, belief, offer clarity and action, not the owner's evidence dossier.
